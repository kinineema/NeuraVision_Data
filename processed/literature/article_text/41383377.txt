Recent advances in therapeutic strategies for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease using protein/peptide co‐modified polymer nanoparticles

AbstractThe blood‐brain barrier (BBB), which protects the brain from foreign molecules, makes delivery of drugs to the central nervous system is challenging. Nanoparticles (NPs) have been used over the past decade as drug delivery systems for the treatment of many disorders with great results. However, the effectiveness of NPs in delivering drugs to the brain for the treatment of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD) is limited by the BBB. A recent breakthrough in nanotechnology delivery systems involves the use of surface‐modified polymer NPs that enhance drug absorption and transport across the BBB; however, the technology still has some limitations. Studies conducted over the past few years have demonstrated that NPs modified with peptides or proteins can effectively cross the BBB via specific receptors, thus enhancing their delivery efficiency. In this review, we explore the use of polymer NPs combined with peptides and proteins for the treatment of AD and PD. This discussion focuses on the pathophysiology of these diseases, the BBB, and the potential of therapeutics based on co‐modifying NPs with peptides and proteins. Additionally, we outline future directions for the use of polymer NPs conjugated with these biomolecules.

The graphical abstract illustrates that drug‐encapsulated polymer nanoparticles are engineered with proteins and peptides to facilitate transport across the blood‐brain barrier through receptor‐mediated transcytosis and delivery of drugs to targeted regions in the brain, thereby offering a more effective treatment for Parkinson's and Alzheimer's diseases. Various peptides and proteins are used in drug delivery systems because they are only focused on a small class of receptors, such as low‐density lipid receptors, Fc‐like growth factor receptors, leptin receptors, receptor‐associated proteins, insulin receptors, scavenger receptor type B1, cell surface receptors, and transferrin receptor. Polymers are crucial for drug delivery because they offer a versatile, efficient, and secure method of improving the administration and effectiveness of therapeutic agents. BBB, blood‐brain barrier.

Highlights Drug‐encapsulated polymer nanoparticles are engineered using proteins and peptides to facilitate transport across the blood‐brain barrier through receptor‐mediated transcytosis and drug delivery to the targeted region in the brain.Polymeric nanoparticles modified with peptides are currently one of the best drug delivery options because of their inherent low toxicity, biocompatibility, controllable degradation, easy elimination, and sustained drug‐release properties, making them suitable vehicles for drug administration.This review suggests that the development of new nanocomposites and their use in clinical practice is within the scope of translational neuroscience.

INTRODUCTION
Neurodegenerative disorders (NDs), such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD), have attracted considerable attention in recent decades because of their high incidence worldwide. 1 , 2 The most prevalent chronic ND worldwide is AD, which affects more than 36 million people. The World Health Organization estimates that this number is expected to reach 115 million by 2050. 3 The number of deaths caused by AD increased in recent years, likely aggravated by the start of the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020. In 2020, more than 11 million family members and unpaid caregivers provided an estimated 15.3 billion hours of care to individuals with AD or other dementias. 4 , 5 AD is typically characterized by memory loss, cognitive impairment, and behavioral changes, due to the presence of ß‐amyloid aggregates, hyperphosphorylated tubulin associated unit (tau) proteins, neurofibrillary tangles, and neuron loss in the brain. 6

AD is distinguished by the presence of amyloid plaques in the brain and the formation of neurofibrillary tangles, which involve tau. AD is believed to be caused by increased the levels of toxic ß‐amyloid oligomers and protofibrils, loss of synaptic connections, and selective neuronal cell death in the brain, which is referred to as the amyloid cascade hypothesis. 7 These amyloid plaques are extracellular, and their main component is ß‐amyloid, which contains 40 amino acid peptides and is generated by sequential cleavage of the amyloid beta precursor protein (APP) by β and α secretases. Abnormal abundance of this natural protein coupling and accumulation of the proteins to form plaques, which collect between neurons and disrupt cell function, leads to AD. 8 Currently, cholinesterase inhibitors, such as donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine and the glutamate antagonist memantine, are the only available drugs that can effectively treat AD. 8 , 9 However, these drugs can only ameliorate the symptoms of AD without curing the disease. Notably, most advancements in the development of therapeutic options for AD have been focused on treatments for preventing or managing its symptoms.

James Parkinson first described PD in 1817 as a “shaking palsy”. PD is the most common age‐ and movement‐related progressive ND worldwide, affecting up to 2%–3% of the global population aged between 65 and 69 years. 10 PD is a progressive ND of the central nervous system (CNS) characterized by progressive denaturation of dopaminergic neurons in the midbrain and substantia nigra, and a decrease in dopamine levels in the striatum. In addition, PD is partly linked to overexpression of α‐synuclein in striatal neurons. 11 , 12 , 13 The characteristic symptoms of PD include shaking and stiffness, as well as challenges with walking, balance, and coordination. The most effective therapeutic agent for the treatment of PD is L‐3,4‐dihydroxyphenylalanine (l‐DOPA); however, its benefits are affected by its rapid and unpredictable metabolic properties, which result in motor fluctuations. 14 The current drug treatments for PD only provide symptomatic relief; they do not prevent the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons and the concomitant decline in motor functions. 15 Thus, new therapeutic agents that are focused on neuroprotection to halt the progression of PD are urgently needed.

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a selectively permeable barrier that lines most of the blood vessels in the brain and is responsible for protecting the CNS against brain infections and neurological disorders. 16 The BBB is mainly composed of endothelial cells connected by tight junctions, which restrict the transfer of molecules and transport of drugs across the barrier. 17 The semi‐permeable structure of the BBB diminishes the pharmacological efficiency of drugs that need to reach targeted sites in the brain, thereby limiting the treatment of brain disorders. Advanced drug delivery systems, such as nano‐carrier drug delivery, have been developed as a state‐of‐the‐art clinical approach towards crossing the BBB. 18 The concept of synthetic nanoparticles (NPs) modified using polymers and peptides or proteins is considered especially promising, flexible, and versatile for the delivery of drugs to inaccessible regions such as the BBB. 19 NPs can act as drug‐delivery vehicles capable of directly manipulating biological targets. Specifically, they can prevent drug degradation, provide continuous drug release, facilitate CNS access, deliver drugs to specific cells, and target specific intracellular pathways.

CONCEPT OF THE BBB
The vascular system plays a crucial role in supplying oxygen and nutrients to all the tissues and organs in the body. At the end of the 19th century, the German doctor Paul Ehrlich discovered that the vascular structures of the CNS have a unique barrier, which was subsequently known as the BBB. 20 , 21 The BBB tightly regulates the passage of various agents, ranging from cells to ions, between the blood stream and the brain. 22 In addition, the BBB allows transport of essential nutrients to the brain and facilitates the maintenance of steady levels of hormones, nutrients, and water. Furthermore, the BBB precisely controls CNS homeostasis, which is necessary for maintaining the stability of neurological functions and protecting the CNS against toxins and pathogens. 23

The BBB is characterized by the absence of intercellular clefts and fenestrae, which limits the passage of substances from the peripheral circulation into the brain. This restricted permeability is primarily due to a specialized system of microvascular endothelial brain cells, which form tight junctions and exhibit low pinocytic activity. 22 , 24 A low‐molecular‐weight substance can be transported across the microvascular endothelial brain cells through passive diffusion. 25 In addition, a large number of lipophilic drug compounds rapidly efflux from the brain through extremely effective drug efflux transport across the BBB. Moreover, potential therapeutic agents such as glycoproteins, multidrug‐resistant proteins, and breast cancer‐resistant and multi‐specific organic anion transporter drugs are limited to brain tissue by the BBB. 26 , 27

The BBB contains enzymatic barriers that regulate drug transport. Brain microvascular endothelial cells selectively transport peptides and small molecules such as amino acids, glucose, vitamins, minerals, electrolytes, and cells across the barrier. 28 Certain polypeptides, including wheat germ agglutinin, leptin, and insulin, cross the BBB via adsorptive transcytosis. 29 The endothelial cell monolayer of cerebral vessels is covered by astrocyte endfeet, which contain parenchymal membranes and contribute to the structure of the BBB. 30 The brain's vasculature is 95% capillaries, which are more permeable to solutes than other capillaries. The epithelial cells of the choroid plexus and other vascular structures of the brain create a strong barrier because the tight junctions between the cells prevent the flow of small molecules and cells into the brain. 31 In addition, paracellular diffusion of hydrophilic molecules into the brain is also limited. Thus, transcellular diffusion is necessary for the transport of most molecules across the BBB (Figure 1). Only small gas molecules or small lipophilic molecules with a molecular weight of less than 400–500 Da (e.g., O2, CO2, nicotine, steroid hormones, and alcohol) and fewer than eight hydrogen bonds can cross the BBB via transmembrane diffusion. 32 , 33 The endothelial cells of the BBB exhibit low levels of pinocytic activity, which further inhibits the brain from receiving molecules. Carrier‐mediated transport by endothelial cells allows for the transport of certain classes of substances, such as amino acids and hexoses, across the BBB. 34 Although many carriers have high affinity for specific molecules, they can often transport nontarget molecules with similar properties. However, carrier‐mediated transport is highly saturable and governed by the specificity and abundance of carrier proteins. Notably, bidirectional carrier‐mediated transport is responsible for regulating homeostasis. Accordingly, carrier proteins mediate the asymmetric efflux of metabolites from the brain, limiting drug delivery to and retention in the brain. 35

Receptor‐mediated transcytosis (RMT) is the only option for transporting molecules from the blood across the BBB (Figure 1). 36 Previous studies have indicated that brain endothelial cells exhibit lower RMT activity than peripheral endothelial cells. Nevertheless, this pathway is promising for drug delivery across the BBB. RMT involves three distinct receptors: insulin, transferrin (Tf), and low‐density lipoprotein. In addition, the roles of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and diphtheria toxin receptors in RMT were described recently. 37 , 38 The RMT mechanism involves three steps: endocytosis, intracellular vesicle trafficking, and exocytosis. Endocytosis is initiated by the binding of molecules to their receptors on the luminal side of endothelial cells. Transport vesicles are formed as a result of the receptor‐ligand complexes invading the cells. Thereafter, the vesicles are sorted for exocytosis, and the ligand is released into the basolateral side of the cell. Finally, the receptor is reused. 39

Apart from transport through the endogenous mechanisms in endothelial cells and other BBB cells, techniques for transient manipulation of the BBB have been explored to enhance drug delivery. Focused ultrasound has been demonstrated to reversibly increase the permeability of the BBB in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex in humans, with no adverse events detected for up to 17 treatments. 40 Osmolarity manipulation has also been used to increase the permeability of the BBB. Chu et al. 12 demonstrated that intra‐arterial injection of hyperosmotic mannitol in mice could permeabilize the BBB, allowing entry of therapeutic agents. Often, localized application of these techniques requires the use of interventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). 41 Pharmaceutical transient opening of the BBB by disrupting its tight junctions or shrinking endothelial cells using agents such as vascular endothelial growth factor 42 and chlorpyrifos 43 has been reported as well. However, transient opening of the BBB is highly variable and potentially dangerous because it can compromise the integrity of the BBB even after recovery of its permeability status, affecting its physiological function. Furthermore, the use of pharmaceutical molecules can lead to the accumulation of unwanted imaging/guiding (like MRI) or permeabilizing agents in the blood, which may be noxious to the CNS at high levels. Moreover, many of these procedures are invasive, which makes them uncomfortable for patients and increases the risk of adverse events. 44 Owing to these limitations, modifying therapeutic NPs to enhance transport across the BBB and brain uptake is a more attractive approach than transient disruption of BBB permeability.

IMPORTANCE OF NPS IN DRUG DELIVERY
NPs are specially designed and synthesized molecules with sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nm. NPs can be grouped into a variety of classes based on their composition, size, or method of action. NPs are composed of metals, often Au, Ag, or Pb, and/or polymers, and can be modified with peptides, lipids, or other biochemical additives for specific functions. NPs are increasingly being favored for therapeutic purposes owing to their customizability; that is, control over their composition, size, and functional groups, which allows for greater specificity, bioavailability, and bioactivity compared to conventional drugs. Furthermore, given that NP coatings allow for control over delivery locations and kinetics, NPs can be tailored to specific patients and diseases. The size and customizability of NPs have made them an increasingly important option for disease treatment. 45 Notably, NPs have been specifically developed to enhance drug delivery for the treatment of NDs.

NPs were developed to enhance the delivery of drugs, including diagnostic agents, to target disease sites and facilitate subsequent release of the drug. In addition, NPs can target specific organs or tissues, 46 including individual cells or intercellular compartments. NPs have an underlying structure integrated with an active drug candidate that allows for angled entry and controlled release of the drug at the targeted site. 47 Traditional modes of drug delivery such as powders, capsules, tablets, or liquids have critical limitations such as high‐dose requirements, short lifetime within the body, and variable pharmacokinetics. However, NPs can successfully transport drugs through the biological acidic milieu with improved permeability, which enables the achievement of maximum efficacy at a relatively low dose. 48 , 49 Considering the physicochemical properties of NPs, even a poorly soluble drug can be effectively translocated to the targeted site to achieve an improved treatment profile. 50 NP‐based technology is considered the best approach to treating diseases due to the myriad of nanomaterials available, which offer numerous positive features for drug delivery strategies. 29 Use of NPs for the treatment of NDs has been gaining attention owing to their improved delivery of drugs and diagnostic agents to diseased target sites and subsequent triggering of drug release.

NPs are characterized by a strong surface adsorption ability. In addition, they have an active center, are highly reactive, and exhibit a high level of catalytic activity. The rates of drug release by NPs can be accurately controlled and modified to improve the therapeutic effects of the delivered drug. Notably, redox effects have been observed in certain NPs, particularly metal and carbon NPs, both in vitro and in vivo. In addition, these NPs have been shown to be potent antioxidants. Antioxidant NPs may be useful as scavengers of reactive oxygen species (ROS). 31 Recent research has proven that NPs can be proactive in bypassing oral and intestinal absorption barriers and delivering drugs to the site of action. This ability can be attributed to the small size of NPs. For instance, drugs with low molecular weights and molecular sizes smaller than 100 nm can pass through the BBB via a diffusion mechanism to avoid phagocytic opsonization, 51 thereby improving their concentration in the brain. Hence, drug‐conjugated nanomaterials have several advantages over modern and traditional bulk materials owing to their special characteristics. 52 The wide range of drug‐delivery properties and therapeutic applications of NPs are discussed in the subsequent sections.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF PD AND CURRENT TREATMENT OPTIONS
The key pathological findings in PD are loss of dopaminergic neurons in basal cells and appearance of Lewy bodies in cell cytosols (Figure 2). Lewy bodies are formed through the aggregation of abnormally folded proteins called α‐synuclein. The discovery of mutations in the SNCA gene led to the identification of α‐synuclein 53 as the underlying reason for the formation of insoluble aggregates from intercellular inclusions within the cell body and Lewy neuritis of neurons. 54 The pathology of the formation of Lewy bodies extends beyond the brain and can be observed in the spinal cord and peripheral nervous system. 55 It is known that α‐synuclein can form a diverse range of aggregate types, including small, dot, thin, and thread‐like structures. 56 α‐synuclein can also be observed as tiny presynaptic deposits and soluble oligomers that contain 2‐110 α‐synuclein monomers. Although Parkinsonism may be influenced by these different forms of α‐synuclein aggregates, oligomeric protofibrils are particularly toxic to neurons. 57 Abnormal and nonfunctional α‐synuclein are eliminated by the autophagy‐lysosomal pathway, the ubiquitin‐proteasome system (UPS), and molecular chaperones. The PTEN‐induced kinase 1 (PINK1), Parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase (PRKN), protein deglycase‐1 (DJ‐1) and ubiquitin C‐terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH‐L1) genes are typically responsible for regulating the UPS. However, if these genes mutate, mitochondrial and UPS dysfunctions will occur, further leading to the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons. 58 , 59

Oxidative stress is a major event that occurs during aging and directly damages the CNS. Free radicals, or ROS, are essential for apoptosis, gene transcription, host defense, and control of neural plasticity under physiological conditions. 60 Oxidative stress occurs when cellular antioxidant activity is surpassed by the activities of ROS. Enhanced oxidative stress resulting from various mechanisms can deplete lysosomes and damage the lysosomal autophagy system. 61 This indicates that oxidative stress is linked to the accumulation of α‐synuclein. Another hypothesis is that excess cytosolic dopamine can be easily oxidized to produce dopamine‐quinones. Furthermore, chaperone‐mediated autophagy may be partially inhibited by α‐synuclein that has been changed by dopamine quinine, leading to α‐synuclein self‐assembly. 62

An important finding that suggests mitochondrial dysfunction plays a role in the etiology of PD was observed in a study conducted in 1983. 63 1‐methyl‐4‐phenyl‐1,2,3,6‐tetrahydropyridine (MPTP), 64 rotenone, 65 , 66 pyridaben, 67 fenpyroximate, 68 and trichloroethylene 69 infusions have been observed to selectively inhibit mitochondrial complex I. Furthermore, animal studies have indicated that mice that overexpress synuclein are more vulnerable to toxins than mice that lack α‐synuclein, suggesting that mitochondrial synuclein increases toxicity. Transcription factors are major causes of mitochondrial dysfunction. An important regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis is the transcription factor coactivator peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma coactivator‐1a (PGC‐1a). 70 Dopaminergic cells in PGC‐1a knockout mice show increased sensitivity to MPTP. Another hypothesis of mitochondrial dysfunction involves mutations in certain genes, including Parkin, DJ‐1, leucine‐rich repeat kinase 2 and PINK1, which are responsible for mitochondrial and UPS function. 71 , 72 , 73 Mutations of these genes cause mitochondrial dysfunction. Another evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction in PD is the increased mitochondrial DNA levels in tissue samples from patients with idiopathic PD. Recent studies have confirmed an increase in mitochondrial DNA deletions in the brains of patients with PD, particularly in post‐mortem substantia nigra pars compacta tissue. 63 , 74

Ferroptosis, also known as iron‐dependent cell death, is caused by abnormal iron metabolism and severe lipid peroxidation. Oxidative stress and cell death are consequences of iron‐dependent controlled cell death. Ferroptosis has been found to be involved in the death of dopamine neurons in patients with PD. 75 Neuroinflammation is a significant factor in the development of PD. The primary controllers of neuronal loss in PD are microglia, which are associated with chronic neuroinflammatory conditions. In response, environmental factors such as MPTP, rotenone, and 6‐hydroxydopamine, including endogenous proteins, can cause overactivation of microglia and produce ROS, which can cause neurotoxicity. 60

PD causes a range of motor and non‐motor symptoms, including tremors, stiffness, slowness of movement, and balance problems. Its non‐motor symptoms include depression, anxiety, and cognitive changes. Treatment helps improve these symptoms, making the performance of daily activities easier. When patients present with motor symptoms, they are typically recommended treatment options that can improve their symptoms and make engagement in daily activities easier. Pharmacological therapy, physical therapy, rehabilitation therapy, and surgery are the therapeutic options currently used for the treatment of PD. The pharmacological treatments focus on relieving motor and non‐motor symptoms. Seven types of medications are available for the treatment of PD: l‐DOPA, carbidopa, dopamine agonists, 76 , 77 monoamine oxidase‐B inhibitors, 78 injectable dopamine agonists, N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate receptor inhibitors, and anticholinergic drugs. These medications are used to stimulate dopamine receptors or inhibit the breakdown of endogenous dopamine. 79 l‐DOPA acts directly as a dopamine receptor agonist and is stable and effective for treating PD. However, its efficacy diminishes with the progression of PD, leading to the onset of motor dysfunction. 80 Current nanotechnology‐based drug delivery techniques focus on relieving motor symptoms of PD by modulating dopamine signaling in the CNS, either through direct delivery of the agent or stimulation of its receptors.

Apart from α‐synuclein pathology, several molecular pathways related to the aforementioned pathophysiology of PD have been identified as potent NP targets for treatment of PD. These include oxidative processes, such as the mitochondrial calcium pathway and Wingless‐related integration site/beta‐catenin pathways. Other pathways that play pivotal roles in PD progression include the protein kinase B/nuclear erythroid 2‐related factor 2/extracellular signal‐regulated kinase/glutathione pathway, which is involved in antioxidant activity, and the platelet‐derived growth factor receptor signaling/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin pathway, which is involved in regulation of the cell cycle. Further, the Janus kinase/signal transducers pathway has been found to be highly involved in the immune activation of microglia and astrocytes in the nervous system. By targeting receptors at the beginning of these pathways or intermediate proteins, NPs can modulate important molecular pathways involved in the progression of PD. 81

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND PRESENT TREATMENT OF AD
The molecular mechanisms underlying the pathology of AD are not fully understood. However, research findings suggest that the amyloid hypothesis explains the primary cause of AD. Paroni et al. 82 separated amyloid β (Aβ) from the brain cortices of patients with AD in 1984. Based on their findings, they suggested for the first time that the accumulation and aggregation of Aβ in the cerebral cortex can cause inherited and non‐inherited AD. 83 This was the first statement on the amyloid hypothesis. In the same year, the authors also discovered that the Aβ amino acid sequence matched the sequence of amino acids extracted from the brain cortices of people with Down syndrome. In 1985, 84 chromosome 21 was discovered at the location of the gene encoding the APP, and in 1987, the APP gene was duplicated at locus 21q21.3. Notably, the amyloid hypothesis has been improved in several aspects since its official declaration in 1992. Aβ deposition is caused by a discrepancy between the production and clearance of Aβ peptide. 82 Gradual accumulation and aggregation of the peptide in the brain is caused by this imbalance, which then leads to a neurodegenerative cascade (Figure 3). 7

APP is best known as the precursor molecule 80 that is formed through sequential cleavages by β and γ secretase at the C and N terminals of Aβ, respectively. β‐secretase generates the 99‐amino acid C‐terminal fragments of APP. Further cleavage is achieved by γ‐secretase at several sites to create cleavage fragments that consist of amino acids 43, 45, 46, 48, 49, and 51. 85 The main final forms of Aβ, including the 40‐amino‐acid Aβ40 and 42‐amino‐acid Aβ42, are further broken down into endocytic compartments. 86 The Aβ peptide causes serious neurotoxin issues and leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and reactions with metal ions in the brain. 87 In addition, it increases oxidative stress, which disrupts calcium homeostasis by changing calcium ion concentrations. Notably, microglia release inflammatory mediators to cause neuroinflammation due to Aβ exposure. Both Aβ and neurotoxin issues are not mutually exclusive as they can trigger each other. Although such response impedes the metabolism of AAP, it results in the accumulation of Aβ peptides. 88 The accumulation of Aβ peptide initiates hyper‐phosphorylation of tau protein, which is a microtubule‐associated protein enclosed by the microtubule‐associated protein tau (MAPT) gene. 89 Tau proteins are abundant in the CNS, and six isoforms are expressed in brain tissue through alternative splicing of the MAPT gene on chromosome number 17q21. In patients with AD, tau is hypo‐phosphorylated and has extra folds compared to normal tau. Tau protein misfolds and accumulates in paired helical filament tau and neurofibrillary tangles. 90 , 91 , 92 Neurofibrillary tangles may reduce normal tau function, compromise normal cellular function, and finally lead to neurodegeneration. 93 , 94

There are two types of treatment options for AD: Nonmedical and medical. Nonmedical treatments primarily aim to enhance quality of life or preserve the patients' cognitive and daily functioning abilities. The Food and Drug Administration has approved six drugs for the treatment of mild‐to‐moderate AD. 95 However, these medications can only temporarily improve the symptoms of the illness. Moreover, the efficacy of the drugs vary significantly between individuals, with patients often reporting side effects such as nausea, diarrhea, and vomiting. 96 Two of these medications, aducanumab and lecanemab, are of a highly pursued class of AD therapies, Aβ‐targeting monoclonal antibodies. Aβ‐targeting antibodies work by binding to Aβ plaques. This binding transforms the plaques into a soluble form, breaking them down and triggering opsonized phagocytosis by glial cells. Both of these antibodies have been demonstrated to decrease Aβ pathology and AD‐associated cognitive deficits in animal models and attenuate Aβ pathology in clinical trials. However, although both antibodies have demonstrated moderate therapeutic effects and an ability to decrease Aβ levels, they have failed to exhibit “clinically significant” benefits in patients with prodromal dementia. In fact, approximately 1.3% of the participants in the EMERGE and ENGAGE clinical trials who were treated with aducanumab developed amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities. 97 Nevertheless, amyloid‐targeting antibodies represent an important culmination in research on the amyloid hypothesis. Further evaluation and improvement of the efficacy of these treatments could position amyloid‐targeting therapeutics as an attractive future treatment option for amyloid‐based dementias. 98 The limitations of the existing therapies highlight the urgent need for alternative therapies for AD. The role of the BBB must be considered in the development of effective treatments for AD. Nanotechnology has significant potential in the development of drug delivery systems with various functional carriers, such as peptides and polymers, that can to alter the properties of drugs to enhance their therapeutic potential for the treatment of AD. 99

In addition to Aβ and Aβ precursor proteins and other protein aggregates such as tau, recent research has identified additional targets for NPs and other therapeutics. These include proteins that control amyloid production and aggregation, such as the medin, bassoon, and pseudoautosomal regions. Recent studies have demonstrated that the pathological forms of these proteins are oligomeric. Thus, oligomeric Aβ and tau may be more specific targets for NPs than previously anticipated. Further, neuroinflammation is increasingly being associated with the incidence and progression of NDs. Thus, NPs and drugs delivered in NPs can be designed to promote an anti‐inflammatory state, such as by incorporating sigma‐1 receptor ligands or by promoting an anti‐inflammatory state in microglia, which are the immune cells of the brain. Research on neuroinflammation‐based therapeutics is currently in the very early stages; however, targeting cytokine receptors, release of cytokines, and other state‐controlling pathways may have potential in the use of NPs for neuroinflammation‐based treatment of AD and PD. 98

PEPTIDES IN DRUG DELIVERY
Many biological processes require the crucial roles of proteins and peptides, such as catalysis, transportation, regulation of genetic expression, and immune functions. Proteins and peptides have diverse functions and are involved in many pathological conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and cancer, making them appealing therapeutic agents for the treatment of these diseases. Currently, there are more than a hundred approved, peptide‐based, therapeutic products in the market. The peptide and protein drug market is growing much faster than that of small‐molecule drugs. This is because peptides and proteins can be very selective as they have several points of interaction with the target. 100

Engineered NPs encapsulated in peptides and proteins that target drug delivery to the brain have been researched in recent decades. Currently, there are a variety of peptides and proteins that are only focused on a small class of receptors, such as low‐density lipid receptors, Fc‐like growth factor receptors, leptin receptors, receptor‐associated proteins, insulin receptors, scavenger receptor type B1, a cell surface receptor, and Tf receptor. 101 Given that these receptors are mostly present in the BBB, receptor‐mediated drug delivery is an important approach to the treatment of AD and PD. 102 Peptides and proteins have been developed as ligands for specific receptors to increase the efficacy of transport across the BBB. NPs encapsulated in peptides and proteins have unique features such as high diversity, specificity, and targeting capability. Therefore, they are used as innovative drug delivery agents for the treatment of AD and PD. 103

The rabies virus glycoprotein‐29 (RGV‐29) is a specific kind of peptide can particularly recognize nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (Table 1). It is not only expressed in endothelial cells but also in astrocytes, microglia, and dopaminergic neurons. Therefore, rabies virus protein 29 encapsulated NP formulations can achieve successful drug delivery to PD lesions. 104 Angiopep‐2 is an oligopeptide that contains 19 amino acids and binds to the low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein‐1 (Table 1). 106 Angiopep‐2 has been encapsulated in different types of nanostructures to effectively deliver therapeutic agents for the treatment of AD and PD. NPs modified with the B6 peptide can accumulate in brain capillary endothelial cells via lipid rafts and clathrin endocytosis (Table 1). B6 has a unique characteristic in that it is capable of dual drug delivery; it is non‐cytotoxic to the mouse hippocampal neuronal cell line (HT‐22) and can cross the BBB both in vitro and in vivo. 108 Tet methylcytosine dioxygenase 1 (Tet‐1) is a peptide with twelve amino acids that was identified by Mathew A et al. 109 through phage display (Table 1). Tet‐1 has transport properties and an affinity for neurons and is used to deliver curcumin, epigallocatechin‐3‐gallate, and small interfering RNA for the treatment of AD. 109 The authors observed a longer lifetime within the blood and greater penetration of the BBB using cyclic Cys‐Arg‐Thr‐IIe‐Gly‐Pro‐Ser‐Val‐Cys (CRT) peptide and conditionally releasable CD47 modified NPs, allowing for more efficient delivery to microglia. RMT has been used to develop long‐circulating delivery drugs, such as dopamine for AD and huperzine for PD, to target certain organs (Table 1). 111

Envelope protein of the rabies virus was developed by the addition of four glycine and nine d‐arginine residues to the C‐terminus peptide and is used to deliver small interfering RNA for the suppression of the α‐synuclein gene involved in the development of PD (Table 1). 113 Human apolipoprotein E (ApoE) receptor‐binding domain is the source of the peptide cysteine‐tryptophan‐glycine‐77, and can be modified by adding the tri‐peptide cysteine‐tryptophan‐glycine at the C‐terminal for the delivery of phosphatidic acid for the treatment of AD (Table 1). 114 The heptapeptide H2N‐Gly‐l‐Phe‐d‐Thr‐Gly‐l‐Phe‐l‐Leu‐l‐Ser(O‐beta‐d‐Glucose)‐CONH2 (g7) has tyrosine at the N‐terminal end and is O‐glycosylated (Table 1). Several in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that g7 effectively transports polyethyleneglycol‐polylactic acid‐co‐glycolic acid NPs into the brain, indicating its potential for the treatment of AD. 115 , 116 The most commonly used cell‐penetrating peptide is Tat peptide, which is used to target AD. Tat peptide contains twelve amino acids and is derived from the protein transduction domain of the human immunodeficiency virus (Table 1). 125

Lactoferrin (Lf) is a natural glycoprotein with iron‐binding properties that is present in large quantities in colostrum and breast milk. In addition, Lf receptors are overexpressed in brain capillary endothelial cells. Owing to the abundance of Lf in these cells, it has been found to be a perfect nano‐carrier for certain drugs such as dopamine and rotigotine for the treatment of PD, and huperzine for the treatment of AD. 126 , 127 The single peptide chain of Tf is a monomeric co‐protein found in blood serum and other human fluids. Tf receptors are expressed in animals, some insects, and bacteria. Tf‐modified liposomal NPs are used for the delivery of ApoE2 for the treatment of AD. 118 Serum albumin is a single‐chain non‐glycosylated, specific structural polypeptide with specific binding sites for a variety of endogenous and exogenous drugs, and is considered the best nano‐carrier for the treatment of AD and PD. 128 Keratin, a protein derived from human hair, has a rich disulfide bond that can be broken in a high‐level reduction environment. In addition, the amino acids in keratin can be protonated in acidic conditions, resulting in pH sensitivity, which bestows the characteristics of dual pH and glutathione responsiveness on the protein. Therefore, keratin is used as a dopamine delivery system in the treatment of PD. 124

PEPTIDE/PROTEIN‐CONJUGATED POLYMERS USED IN DRUG DELIVERY FOR THE TREATMENT OF AD AND PD
Polymeric NPs (PNPs) are particles made of polymers with sizes ranging from 1 nm to 1000 nm. 129 Degradable NPs are increasingly being utilized for the delivery of drugs for the treatment of NDs, owing to their high loading capacity, ability to permeate the BBB to reach the CNS, 130 minimal toxic properties, and controllable degradation timeline and degrees. 131 PNPs are block copolymers composed of naturally occurring monomers, such as polysaccharides and albumin, as well as synthetic monomers. They are easily excreted, biodegradable, and largely biocompatible, and thus suitable for human biological applications. 132 Polymeric nano‐capsules are another form of polymer NPs in which the drug is encapsulated in the internal cavity created by a polymer coating. 133 Like PNPs, polymer‐based NPs vary in retention time, solubility, and biological availability, making them attractive options as therapeutic agents. However, the nature of the interactions between the polymer and the drug should be considered in applications of encapsulation. If the interaction between the polymer and drug is more dominant than the interactions between the molecules of the drug, the drug can be stably incorporated into NPs. However, if the drug–drug interaction is stronger, drug crystallization occurs. To optimize the balance of these interactions, various natural polymers, such as albumin, 134 alginate, 135 gelatin, 136 and chitosan, 77 and synthetic polymers such as poly (lactic‐co‐glycolic) acid (PLGA), 137 , 138 poly (lactic acid), 139 poly (caprolactone), 140 , 141 poly (butyl cyanoacrylate), 142 , 143 , 144 and poly‐l‐lactic acid, 145 are being tested for medical applications. Use of PNPs is one of the most promising and modern strategies for delivery of therapeutic drugs to the brains of patients with AD. The superior internal distribution and pharmacokinetics of these agents hold great promise for improving the efficacy of drug delivery. The most commonly selected polymers for the synthesis of NPs are chitosan, albumin, poly (butyl cyanoacrylate), poly (lactic acid), PLGA, polyethylene glycol, and polyethylenimine. These polymers contain moieties that facilitate the formation of three‐dimensional nanonetworks through covalent cross‐linking with other motifs of the same polymer backbone, leading to single polymer‐based or single‐chain polymer NPs. 146 In addition, the polymers react with other polymers to form heterogeneous NPs. Groups that are not utilized for the assembly or synthesis of the NPs can be employed for functionality, including controlled release and localization of drugs. Drugs can be restricted from being released through many chemical interactions, including steric hindrance such as covalent bonding or attachment to the surface of the NP, allowing for versatile dispersion and absorption of drugs. 147 In addition to temporal control, various chemical gating and attachments allow the release of drugs to specific areas of the brain. These functional modifications allow for efficient loading of drugs, as well as controlled release and selective targeting of specific cell types or brain regions. 148

Fan et al. 108 designed a novel NP, poly (lactide‐co‐glycolide)‐block‐poly (ethylene glycol) (PLGA‐PEG), which they conjugated with peptide B6, loaded with curcumin (PLGA‐PEG‐B6/Cur), and administered to HT‐22 cells and APP/PS1 Al transgenic mice (Table 2). The developed NPs were subjected to in vitro tests, including dynamic light scattering, flow cytometry red blood cell lysis, and thromboelastographic analysis. A result from another study suggested that the PLGA‐PEG‐B6/Cur NP exhibits high biosafety and availability and can be used to treat β‐amyloidosis and tau pathology. Comparison of APP/PS1 mice treated with the NP with mice that received a curcumin treatment demonstrated that the NP treatment improved spatial learning and memory in the disease model. In addition, ex vivo analysis of samples from these mice, including immunostaining, western blot analysis, and Bielschowsky silver staining, demonstrated that PLGA‐PEG‐B6/Cur can reduce the deposition and formation of β‐amyloid as well as hyperphosphorylation. Furthermore, behavioral and biochemical tests revealed the ability of the PLGA‐PEG‐B6/Cur NP to serve as an a therapeutic agent for AD, augmented by the increased delivery to the brain by the PLGA‐PEG‐B6 formulation. 108

Liu et al. 149 developed B6‐modified PEG‐PLA NPs as an effective drug delivery system for improving neuroprotective drug delivery across the BBB for the treatment of AD (Table 2). The ability of the B6 peptide to increase the localization of the NPs in the brain was studied using real‐time imaging. After the NPs were labeled with 1,1′‐dioctadecyl‐3,3,3′,3′‐tetramethyl indotricar‐bocyanine iodide and injected in veins in the tails of mice, the fluorescence of the B6‐NPs lasted longer in the brain than that of the unmodified NPs at all timepoints from 0.5 to 12 h following administration. In fact, the fluorescence of the B6‐NPs was higher than that of the other NPs 1 h after administration, and greater accumulation of B6‐NPs than the unmodified NPs was observed in vivo. At the cellular level, B6‐NP demonstrated endocytosis via lipid rafts and clathrin mechanisms, resulting in significantly high accumulation in endothelial cells. The B6‐NPs were encapsulated in the neuroprotective peptide Asp‐Ala‐Pro‐Val‐Ser‐IIe‐Pro‐1 (NAP) to generate an NP for treatment of AD. When B6‐NP was administered to AD mouse models, NAP mice showed reduced learning disabilities, hippocampal neuron death, and cholinergic signaling disruption, even at low doses. 149

In another study, Bi et al. 120 proposed lactoferrin‐modified PEG‐PLGA NPs as an adjunct to rotigotine for the treatment of PD (Table 2). Cellular uptake of the Lf‐NPs in human bronchial epithelial cells (16 HBE) and SH‐SY5Y cells demonstrated higher accumulation of the Lf‐NPs than unmodified NPs. Intranasal administration of rotigotine to the brain was heterogeneous, yet more effective with Lf‐NPs. Specifically, the Lf‐NPs were highly concentrated in the striatum. Given the susceptibility of striatal neurons to degeneration, this finding suggests that Lf‐NPs allow for the delivery of rotigotine to the most relevant areas in the brain for the treatment of PD. 120

Vilella et al. 116 studied the effects of elevated brain zinc levels on AD pathology in mice treated with g7‐NPs‐Zinc (g7‐NP‐Zn). The authors developed zinc‐containing NPs and performed in vivo studies using wild‐type and APP23 mice to assess plaque burden, inflammatory status, and synapse loss. After 14 days of chromic injections, the mice showed significantly fewer pro‐inflammatory cytokines and smaller plaque sizes. No negative behavioral consequences of elevated brain zinc levels were observed in the APP‐23 mice. In addition, the g7‐NP‐Zn treatment normalized the observed hyperlocomotion in the APP23 mice (Table 2). 116

Huang et al. 112 designed PLGA NPs by coating the amyloid beta generation inhibitor S1 peptide and curcumin to target deleterious factors in patients with AD using the brain‐targeting conjugation CRT peptide (Table 2). The authors assessed spatial memory and recognition using object recognition and Y‐maze tests and found that treatment with the PLGA NPs improved these skills in transgenic AD mice, while simultaneously reducing the amount of ROS and pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including necrosis factor‐alpha and interleukin‐6. In addition, these NPs increased the levels of protective indicators, including superoxide dismutase, and synaptic activities in the mice. Coating with the CRT peptide showed the most favorable treatment of AD compared to other S1 and curcumin‐delivering PLGA NPs. 112 Yan et al. 117 presented an intranasal strategy mediated by PLGA‐modified cell‐penetrating peptide NPs for the delivery of insulin to the brain to treat AD. The authors performed a cellular reception efficacy test using Caco‐2 cells. In addition, they performed fluorescence‐activated cell sorting analysis and reported that Tat‐NPs greatly increased the efficacy of intracellular insulin delivery, showing 4.5 times more accumulation compared with unmodified NPs. Confocal imaging also confirmed the greater intracellular accumulation of insulin. The results indicated that when administered nasally, Tat‐NPs can deliver insulin to the brain with an overall efficacy of 6%. 117 Monge‐Fuentes et al. 123 studied dopamine‐loaded albumin/PLGA nanosystems in 6‐hydroxydopamine ‐induced PD mice models and revealed that at the highest dose, dopamine encapsulated in albumin/PLGA nanosystems significantly improved neurobehavioral symptoms (Figure 4). The improved symptoms included motor coordination, sensory‐motor performance, and balance. In addition, the results indicated that 20 mg of aluminum chloride phthalocyanine can both deliver and replenish dopamine in a manner that translates to tangible effects on PD symptoms. 123

Dos Santos Rodrigues et al. 118 designed liposomal NPs that can protect the ApoE2 plasmid from nuclease degradation (Table 2). A Tf‐modified liposome surface‐penetrating agent effectively induced ApoE expression in vitro by delivering plasmid DNA to the gene. When tested in vivo, the Tf‐penetratin liposome was able to induce ApoE expression in the brains of mice compared to nontargeted preparations. These results demonstrate the great potential of liposomes for the delivery of plasmid DNA and their applications in the treatment of NDs, including AD. 118 Guo et al. 150 developed double‐PEG‐modified single‐walled carbon nanotubes with Lf on their surfaces (SWCNT‐PEGs‐Lf or CAR), which transport dopamine to the brain. Treatment of parkinsonian mice with 25 mg/kg of CAR and dopamine reduced the levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (interleukin‐1b, tumor necrosis factor‐α, and ROS), while increasing the density of the immunoreactive tyrosine hydroxylase. Analysis of the doses of CAR and DA that elicited lower oxidative stress and pro‐inflammatory responses following intraperitoneal injection revealed that 25 mg kg‐1 dopamine on 3.25 mg/kg CAR can rescue the aforementioned features in parkinsonian mice. 150

In another study, Zhang et al. 110 demonstrated the process of Aβ aggregation and disaggregation of amyloid fibrils through attachment to epigallocatechin‐3‐gallate (EGCG) to the surface selenium NPs (epigallocatechin‐3‐gallate @ selenium [EGCG@Se]) (Table 2). They further synthesized EGCG‐stabilized selenium NPs coated with Tet‐1 peptide (Tet‐1‐EGCG@Se, a synthetic analog of sialoprotein), with respect to the affinity of Tet‐1 peptide to neurons (Figure 5). The results of the study showed that Tet‐1‐EGCG@Se can inhibit Aβ fibrillation while disaggregating toxic Aβ fibrils. Moreover, the authors found that both EGCG@Se and Tet‐1‐EGCG@Se can label Aβ fibrils and Tet‐1 peptides, increasing uptake of Tet‐1‐EGCG@Se in PC12 cells. 110 Tang et al. 121 prepared NPs co‐modified with borneol and Lf and loaded them with dopamine using a double‐emulsion solvent evaporation method. The results of in vitro cytotoxicity studies suggested that treatment with dopamine borneol and Lf co‐modified NPs (Lf‐BNPs) is not significantly cytotoxic to SH‐SY5Y and 16HBE cells (Table 1). The authors also found that the Lf modification increased the uptake of NPs by SH‐SY5Y and 16HBE cells, whereas addition of borneol promoted uptake by 16HBE cells. The results also indicated that the Lf‐NPs had a significantly higher area under the curve from 0 to 12 h than dopamine NPs. Intranasal administration of dopamine effectively attenuated 6‐hydroxydopamine‐induced Lf‐BNP lesions in the striatum of rats, as indicated by the results of a contralateral rotation behavioral test and the detection of striatal monoamine neurotransmitter content. In addition to the previously discussed NPs, intranasal administration of dopamine Lf‐BNPs is an effective method for improving the symptoms of PD. 121

Based on the results of their study, the authors validated Lf‐GO‐Pue as safe and effective therapeutic agent for the treatment of PD symptoms. 151 Gothwal et al. 122 used a lower‐generation PAMAM lactoferrin conjugate (PAMAM‐Lf) loaded with memantine (MEM) for the treatment of AD (Figure 6). FUTURE DIRECTIONS IN THE APPLICATION OF POLYMER NPS CONJUGATED WITH PEPTIDES/PROTEINS
Polymer‐based NPs have emerged as well‐established drug delivery systems because of their significant advantages over other systems. The therapeutic potential and physical properties of NPs are determined by their monomer composition and preparation process. Many synthetic and natural polymers have been used for the synthesis of protein and peptide nanosystems. The high activity, circulation time, specificity, and low toxicity of these polymer–protein systems make them ideal candidates as nanocarriers. However, the development of polymer–protein hybrid systems is still faced with some challenges. Polymeric nanomaterials must be evaluated and modulated to minimize in vivo toxicity, which can be affected by their physical properties, such as chemical composition, shape, and size, their biochemical properties including agglomeration, surface properties, and their biological properties, such as biocompatibility and biodegradability. In addition to the use of organic solvents, covalent or noncovalent modifications can change the structure of a protein. Therefore, conformation and activity of the protein should be determined during the synthesis procedure to confirm the feasibility of the method. Recent developments in polymer chemistry research involved the reconstruction of synthetic processes and applications of polymer NPs. This has led to the successful development of several new formulations of therapeutic peptides with suitable sizes and surface chemistries. The interaction between the peptides and polymer backbones can be adjusted by considering more factors in the preliminary preparation step. Drug release kinetics can be adjusted and controlled, and the initial burst of drug release can be prevented. Peptide encapsulation of drugs and relevant stabilizing agents in polymer nanosystems can significantly improve peptide stability during formulation and delivery. The rational design of peptide‐conjugated PNPs should involve deep modifications of the sophisticated physicochemical properties of the biological systems and target areas. However, it should be noted that delivery of a pharmacologically active peptide is challenging. Future research should be focused on safe and effective noninvasive administration of PNPs in biological systems. In addition, cooperation and interaction among researchers in various fields, such as chemistry, materials science, biology, and medicine, will enable informal development of versatile polymer NP conjugates to overcome the limitations of the existing therapeutic options.

Peptide‐modified NPs can be further modified to enable visualization of the particles following treatment. Particles may be fluorescently labeled through covalent or noncovalent attachment of a fluorescent molecule to the NP. Fluorophores, such as fluorescent quantum dots, which are especially bright, have narrow/specific emission spectra, and are very photostable, can be encapsulated inside NPs. Furthermore, Forster resonance energy transfer techniques can be used to detect interactions between NPs and their microenvironments through pH‐ or distance‐dependent energy transfer. In addition, NPs can be radiolabeled to detect particles via isotope decay. Some radioactive isotopes can be covalently attached; however, many are immobilized on the surface of NPs, which poses a risk of detachment from the particle following intake. NPs can also be radiolabeled without a chelator through de novo synthesis by adding radioactive precursors during the synthesis of inorganic materials, allowing for incorporation from the beginning. Alternatively, chemical adsorption, in which NPs adsorb radioactive ions from the solution onto their surfaces, can be performed. Finally, non‐metallic NPs can be labeled with metals using various techniques, to enable detection through various imaging techniques, including MRI and transmission electron microscopy. Metal tags can be incorporated onto the surfaces and cores of NPs, allowing for detection without interference with the function of the NP or its interaction with other molecules. 152

CONCLUSION
The number of individuals with NDs has been increasing in recent decades. NDs, such as AD and PD, have significant socioeconomic impacts worldwide, primarily because of the progressive aging of populations and the lack of effective therapies for the diseases. The conventional therapeutic approach to the management of NDs has multiple limitations owing to physiological factors and the complexity of the diseases. Despite several research developments, the existing treatments for AD and PD are not effective because of gaps between research findings and proper implementation in clinical settings. The ability of nanotechnology‐based therapeutic agents to selectively target the brain has provided an innovative way to treat these diseases more specifically and efficiently. Recent advances in nanotechnology offer new ways of boosting research on the delivery of targeted drugs to specific sites in the brain. However, despite the availability of promising therapeutic materials, their limited delivery to the brain hinders their clinical applicability. The BBB makes it challenging to identify permeable points of access to the brain and CNS from the blood. This review demonstrates that nanotechnology offers a novel method for addressing this problem, with an abundance of evidence indicating that among various controlled‐release systems, modification of PNPs with peptides is one of the best options. Owing to their inherent low toxicity, controllable degradation and elimination, and biocompatibility, modified PNPs are suitable for drug administration. However, PNPs have a high loading capacity and can be easily altered while targeting and penetrating molecules. Polymer‐based NPs offer a controlled mechanism for drug delivery and treatment in nanomedicine. The inherent adaptability of these systems highlight their potential as novel alternatives for the treatment of many neurological diseases. In addition, preclinical studies have provided insights into many alternative treatment options.
