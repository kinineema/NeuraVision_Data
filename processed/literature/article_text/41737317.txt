Next questions of autophagy in neurodegenerative diseases: From mechanisms to therapeutics

Autophagy, a key cellular degradation pathway, is central to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Despite progress in understanding its role, critical questions remain. This perspective highlights pressing issues, including cell-type-specific autophagy regulation, interactions with other cellular pathways, and challenges in translating autophagy-modulating therapies to clinical practice. Addressing these questions will advance our understanding of neurodegenerative diseases and pave the way for novel therapeutics.

Introduction
Neurodegenerative diseases are characterized by progressive neuronal loss and toxic protein aggregate accumulation.1 Autophagy, a critical cellular degradation pathway, plays a central role in these disorders.2 However, several key questions remain unanswered. This perspective aims to address these questions, focusing on cell-type-specific autophagy regulation, interactions with other cellular pathways, and challenges in translating autophagy-modulating therapies to clinical practice. Recent studies have highlighted the dual roles of autophagy in promoting and inhibiting inflammation in microglia, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of these mechanisms.

Cell-type-specific roles of autophagy in neurodegeneration
Autophagy plays distinct roles in neurons, astrocytes, and microglia.2 Neurons, with high metabolic demand and limited cell division capacity, are particularly vulnerable to misfolded proteins and damaged organelles. Autophagy in neurons maintains synaptic function and prevents toxic aggregate accumulation, as seen in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) models.3,4 Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cells, support neuronal metabolism and modulate neuroinflammation.5 Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, are key players in neuroinflammation and synaptic pruning.6 Dysregulated autophagy in microglia can lead to chronic inflammation and impaired debris clearance.7

Selective autophagy pathways degrade specific cellular components, maintaining homeostasis. Mitophagy, which targets damaged mitochondria, is crucial for neuronal survival, with impaired mitophagy linked to increased oxidative stress in neurodegenerative diseases.8 Aggrephagy, targeting protein aggregates, is relevant in AD and PD.9 Other pathways like lipophagy and ribophagy also contribute to cellular homeostasis. Cell-type-specific autophagy defects drive disease progression. In neurons, impaired autophagy leads to toxic aggregate accumulation and synaptic dysfunction. In astrocytes and microglia, autophagy dysfunction exacerbates neuroinflammation and impairs supportive functions.5 Understanding the cell-type-specific roles of autophagy faces challenges: brain cell heterogeneity, the dynamic nature of autophagy, and limited access to human brain tissue. Single-cell omics, advanced imaging techniques, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), and brain organoids offer solutions. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) identifies cell-type-specific autophagy-related genes, while advanced imaging visualizes autophagy dynamics in live cells and tissues, iPSCs and brain organoids model autophagy in a humanized context.

Interplay between autophagy and other cellular pathways in neurodegeneration
Autophagy interacts with other cellular processes, including the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, apoptosis, mitochondrial dynamics, inflammation, and lysosomal function. Understanding these interactions is crucial for identifying therapeutic targets. The UPS degrades short-lived and misfolded proteins,10 with autophagy compensating when the UPS is overwhelmed. Crosstalk involves molecular players like Parkin and SQSTM1. ER stress induces autophagy to degrade misfolded proteins. Key signaling molecules include PERK, ATF6, and IRE1. Autophagy and apoptosis are interconnected, with proteins like BNIP3 and Beclin-1 influencing both pathways. Mitophagy driven by the PINK1/Parkin pathway degrades damaged mitochondria. Impaired mitophagy contributes to oxidative stress in neurodegenerative diseases. Autophagy modulates inflammation by degrading pro-inflammatory molecules like nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and regulating inflammasomes. Chronic inflammation impairs autophagy. Lysosomal dysfunction impairs autophagy, leading to undegraded substrate accumulation and autophagosome persistence.

Genetic studies have provided substantial evidence linking autophagy-lysosome pathways to neurodegenerative diseases. Mutations in genes encoding key components of these pathways have been identified in patients with AD, PD, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). For example, mutations in the PINK1 and Parkin genes, which are involved in mitophagy, are associated with familial forms of PD. Similarly, mutations in the ATG5 and ATG7 genes, which are essential for autophagy, have been linked to neurodegenerative phenotypes in animal models. These genetic findings highlight the critical role of autophagy-lysosome pathways in maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing neurodegeneration.

The interplay between autophagy and other cellular pathways has significant implications for disease pathology. For example, the crosstalk between autophagy and the UPS can influence the clearance of protein aggregates and the maintenance of protein homeostasis. Impaired UPS function can lead to the accumulation of misfolded proteins, which in turn activates autophagy. However, if autophagy is also dysregulated, the clearance of these aggregates can be compromised, leading to the progression of neurodegenerative diseases.10 Similarly, the interaction between autophagy and ER stress can affect the handling of misfolded proteins and the maintenance of ER homeostasis. Chronic ER stress can induce autophagy, but if autophagy is insufficient to degrade the misfolded proteins, they can aggregate and contribute to neuronal dysfunction and death. The crosstalk between autophagy and inflammation can also influence disease progression. Chronic inflammation can impair autophagy function, leading to the accumulation of cellular debris and the persistence of inflammation, which further damages neurons.

In AD, Aβ and tau aggregates impair autophagy by disrupting lysosomal function and microtubule dynamics. In PD, mutations in PINK1 and Parkin impair mitophagy, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Alpha-synuclein aggregates inhibit autophagy. In ALS, mutations in TDP-43 and FUS disrupt autophagy, impairing autophagosome-lysosome fusion and contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction. Modulating autophagy interactions with other pathways offers therapeutic opportunities. Enhancing UPS function, targeting ER stress, reducing inflammation, and improving mitochondrial dynamics and lysosomal function can provide synergistic benefits. Systems biology approaches and multi-omics integration can identify regulatory networks and therapeutic targets.

Temporal and spatial dynamics of autophagy in neurodegenerative diseases
Understanding the temporal and spatial dynamics of autophagy is crucial for elucidating its role in neurodegenerative diseases and developing effective therapies. Advanced biosensors and longitudinal imaging studies are needed to visualize autophagy in live cells and tissues, providing insights into its role in disease progression. The activity of autophagy can change during the progression of neurodegenerative diseases. In the early stages of diseases such as AD and PD, autophagy may be upregulated as a protective response to the accumulation of misfolded proteins and damaged organelles. However, as the disease progresses, autophagy can become impaired, leading to the accumulation of toxic aggregates and neuronal dysfunction. For example, studies have shown that autophagy markers such as LC3-II and SQSTM1 are upregulated in the brains of patients with early-stage AD but become dysregulated in late-stage patients. This suggests that autophagy dysfunction may be a hallmark of disease progression.

Autophagy defects may be more detrimental in specific brain regions or neuronal circuits. For instance, in AD, the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex are among the earliest regions affected by the disease, and autophagy dysfunction in these regions may contribute to memory impairment and cognitive decline. Similarly, in PD, the substantia nigra is particularly vulnerable to the accumulation of alpha-synuclein aggregates, and impaired autophagy in this region may lead to the loss of dopaminergic neurons. Understanding the regional specificity of autophagy defects can provide insights into the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases and inform the development of targeted therapies.

Developing tools to monitor autophagy dynamics in vivo in real time is a critical challenge. Traditional methods to study autophagy, such as immunohistochemistry and western blotting, provide static snapshots of autophagy activity but do not capture its dynamic nature. Advanced biosensors and imaging techniques are needed to visualize autophagy in live cells and tissues. For example, genetically encoded fluorescent reporters, such as mRFP-GFP-LC3 and mCherry-SQSTM1, can be used to monitor autophagy in real time in animal models. Additionally, longitudinal imaging studies using positron emission tomography (PET) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide insights into the temporal and spatial dynamics of autophagy in human patients.

Innovative experimental models are essential for capturing the temporal and spatial dynamics of autophagy in different neurodegenerative conditions. These models should be designed to reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of human diseases. For example, the use of iPSCs derived from patients with neurodegenerative diseases can provide a platform for studying autophagy in a disease-relevant context. Additionally, three-dimensional (3D) brain organoids can be used to model the spatial dynamics of autophagy in a more physiologically relevant environment. These models can be combined with advanced imaging techniques to monitor autophagy dynamics over time and in different brain regions.

Translating autophagy modulation into effective therapies
Translating autophagy-modulating therapies into clinical practice remains challenging. Key issues include specificity, delivery, heterogeneity, and long-term safety. Achieving selective modulation of autophagy in specific cell types or brain regions is essential for minimizing off-target effects and maximizing therapeutic efficacy. For example, enhancing autophagy in neurons may be beneficial for clearing toxic aggregates, but indiscriminate activation of autophagy in other cell types may have unintended consequences. Strategies to achieve selective autophagy modulation include the use of cell-type-specific promoters to drive the expression of autophagy-inducing genes or the development of small molecules that selectively target autophagy pathways in specific cell types.

Long-term activation or inhibition of autophagy may have potential side effects. For example, chronic activation of autophagy can lead to the degradation of essential cellular components, resulting in cellular dysfunction and toxicity. Conversely, long-term inhibition of autophagy can lead to the accumulation of damaged organelles and misfolded proteins, exacerbating neurodegenerative pathology. Understanding the potential side effects of autophagy modulation is crucial for developing safe and effective therapies. Identifying biomarkers to stratify patients who would benefit most from autophagy-targeted therapies is essential for personalized medicine. Biomarkers can help predict patient responses to therapy and monitor disease progression. For example, levels of autophagy markers such as LC3-II and SQSTM1 in cerebrospinal fluid or peripheral blood can be used to assess autophagy activity in patients. Additionally, genetic biomarkers, such as mutations in autophagy-related genes, can identify patients with impaired autophagy function who may benefit from autophagy-modulating therapies.

There are major obstacles to clinical translation, including the following: (1) many autophagy-modulating compounds lack specificity, leading to off-target effects. (2) The blood-brain barrier restricts drug entry. Targeted drug delivery systems like nanoparticles and liposomes can enhance brain delivery. (3) Neurodegenerative diseases are highly heterogeneous, requiring personalized medicine approaches. (4) Long-term studies are needed to assess the safety and efficacy of autophagy-modulating therapies.

Conclusion
Autophagy research has significantly advanced our understanding of neurodegenerative diseases, but many critical questions remain. Addressing these questions will require interdisciplinary approaches, combining cutting-edge technologies with innovative experimental models. By unraveling the complexities of autophagy in neurodegeneration, we can identify novel therapeutic targets and ultimately improve outcomes for patients with these devastating disorders. Future research should focus on several key areas to further our understanding and develop effective therapies for neurodegenerative diseases (Figure 1): (1) Investigating the cell-type-specific roles of autophagy in neurons, astrocytes, and microglia is crucial. Single-cell omics and advanced imaging techniques, such as scRNA-seq and super-resolution microscopy, can provide detailed insights into the gene expression profiles and dynamics of autophagy in different cell types; (2) The crosstalk between autophagy and other cellular pathways, such as the UPS, ER stress, apoptosis, mitochondrial dynamics and inflammation, plays a significant role in neurodegenerative diseases. Enhancing our understanding of these interactions will provide new therapeutic opportunities; (3) Monitoring the temporal and spatial dynamics of autophagy in vivo is essential for understanding its role in disease progression. Advanced biosensors and imaging techniques, such as genetically encoded fluorescent reporters and multiphoton microscopy, can provide real-time information on autophagy activity in live cells and tissues. Longitudinal imaging studies in animal models and human patients can offer valuable insights into these dynamics; and (4) Translating autophagy-modulating therapies into clinical practice remains challenging. Achieving selective modulation of autophagy in specific cell types or brain regions is essential to minimize off-target effects and maximize therapeutic efficacy. Developing small molecules, gene therapies, and targeted drug delivery systems that precisely target autophagy pathways will be crucial for clinical translation.
